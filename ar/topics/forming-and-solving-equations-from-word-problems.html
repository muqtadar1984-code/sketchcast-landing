الشرح

Translating Words into Algebra

Word problems present mathematical relationships in everyday language rather than symbolic notation. To solve them, one must bridge the gap between English sentences and algebraic expressions. The first and most crucial step in this process is defining a variable. A variable is an unknown quantity, usually represented by a letter such as x, n, or y, that changes or stands in place of an unknown value. Choosing a clear letter and writing down what it represents prevents confusion later.

Key phrases in word problems translate directly into mathematical operations. Addition terms include sum, increased by, more than, and combined. Subtraction terms include difference, decreased by, fewer than, and less than. Multiplication involves product, times, of, and twice or triple. Division involves quotient, split, per, and shared equally. The word "is" or "equals" almost always translates to the equals sign, representing balance between two quantities.

Consider the phrase "five more than three times a number is twenty-two." Letting x represent the unknown number, "three times a number" becomes 3x. "Five more than" that quantity translates to adding 5, giving 3x + 5. The word "is" becomes equals, and twenty-two is 22. The entire sentence becomes the equation 3x + 5 = 22. Careful reading ensures that operations apply in the correct order, respecting the structure of the written sentence.

Constructing Equations from Contexts

Once individual phrases are understood, entire scenarios can be modelled as equations. Many practical situations involve a fixed base cost or starting amount combined with a variable rate that depends on quantity. For instance, a taxi journey might charge a fixed call-out fee plus an amount per kilometre travelled. If the fixed fee is four pounds and the rate is two pounds per kilometre, the total cost for k kilometres is represented by 2k + 4.

When a word problem compares two quantities that are equal, or describes a total resulting from combined parts, an equation is formed. Consider perimeter problems. The perimeter of a rectangle is calculated by doubling the sum of its length and width. If a rectangle has a length that is three centimetres greater than its width, and the total perimeter is thirty-eight centimetres, defining the width as w makes the length w + 3. The perimeter expression is 2 times (w + w + 3), which simplifies to 4w + 6. Setting this equal to 38 yields the equation 4w + 6 = 38.

Another common context is consecutive number problems. Consecutive integers follow one another in order, such as 12, 13, and 14. If the first integer is x, the next consecutive integer is x + 1, and the third is x + 2. The sum of three consecutive integers is represented by x + (x + 1) + (x + 2), which simplifies to 3x + 3. Constructing equations from these varied contexts relies on identifying the unknown, establishing the relationships between quantities, and ensuring both sides of the equation represent the exact same value in different forms.

Solving Linear Equations

Solving an equation derived from a word problem requires isolating the variable using inverse operations. The principle of balancing equations dictates that whatever operation is performed on one side must be performed on the other side to maintain equality. Working backwards through the order of operations is the standard strategy for linear equations.

To solve an equation in the form ax + b = c, the first step is to eliminate the constant term b by applying the opposite operation to both sides. If b is added, subtract b from both sides. Next, eliminate the coefficient a by dividing both sides by a, or multiplying by its reciprocal if a is a fraction. For equations containing brackets, expand the brackets first using the distributive law, or divide by the outer factor if appropriate, before gathering like terms.

When variables appear on both sides of the equation, such as in separation or meeting problems, the goal is to collect all variable terms on one side and all constant terms on the other. This is achieved by adding or subtracting variable terms from both sides until only one side contains the variable. Checking the solution by substituting the final value back into the original equation ensures accuracy and confirms that the arithmetic was executed without error.

Interpreting Solutions and Context

Finding the numerical value of the variable is only part of the process; the final answer must be interpreted within the context of the original word problem. A purely mathematical solution might be a negative number, a fraction, or a decimal, but the physical reality of the problem may restrict what values are valid. For example, if a variable represents the number of people on a bus or the length of a wooden plank, negative numbers and illogical decimals must be rejected.

Units of measurement must be attached to the final answer. If a problem asks for length in centimetres, the solution should explicitly state centimetres, not just a bare number. Furthermore, some word problems ask for more than just the value of the variable. If a variable x is defined as the age of the younger sibling, and the question asks for the age of the older sibling who is x + 5, finding x is only an intermediate step. The final step requires substituting x back into the expression for the older sibling.

Checking the answer against the words of the original problem provides a final verification. If the problem states that the sum of two numbers is 45 and one is twice the other, the derived numbers must actually add up to 45 and one must be double the other. Interpreting the solution bridges abstract algebra and tangible reality, confirming that the mathematical model accurately reflects the physical situation.

Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

Learners frequently encounter specific pitfalls when forming and solving equations from word problems. One common error is defining the variable poorly, such as using multiple different letters for the same unknown quantity or failing to state what the variable represents. Always assign a single letter to the primary unknown and express any other unknown quantities in terms of that same letter.

  • Misinterpreting phrase direction: Phrases like "5 less than x" are often written incorrectly as 5 - x instead of the correct x - 5. Subtraction is non-commutative, so the order matters. The item appearing after "less than" or "subtracted from" comes first in the algebraic expression.
  • Forgetting distribution: When multiplying a bracketed expression by a number, learners sometimes multiply only the first term inside the bracket and forget the second term. Every term inside the parentheses must be multiplied by the outside factor.
  • Ignoring context limits: Stopping the calculation as soon as the variable value is found without answering the actual question asked. Always reread the final question to ensure the output matches what was requested, including correct units.

Worked Examples

The following worked examples demonstrate the complete process of forming an equation from a word problem, solving it systematically, and interpreting the final result.

Example 1: Age Problem

Sarah is three times as old as her brother Tom. In four years' time, the sum of their ages will be 38. How old are they now?

Let Tom's current age be x. Therefore, Sarah's current age is 3x. In four years, Tom's age will be x + 4, and Sarah's age will be 3x + 4. The sum of their ages in four years is (x + 4) + (3x + 4) = 38. Simplifying the left side gives 4x + 8 = 38. Subtracting 8 from both sides yields 4x = 30. Dividing by 4 gives x = 7.5. Interpreting this, Tom is currently 7.5 years old, and Sarah is 3 times 7.5, which is 22.5 years old.

Example 2: Perimeter Problem

The length of a rectangular garden is 5 metres longer than its width. If the total perimeter of the garden is 34 metres, find its dimensions.

Let the width of the garden be w metres. The length is w + 5 metres. The formula for perimeter is 2 times (length + width), so 2(w + (w + 5)) = 34. Simplifying inside the bracket gives 2(2w + 5) = 34. Expanding the brackets gives 4w + 10 = 34. Subtracting 10 from both sides gives 4w = 24. Dividing by 4 gives w = 6. The width is 6 metres, and the length is 6 + 5 = 11 metres. Checking, 2 times (11 + 6) equals 34 metres.

Example 3: Cost and Quantity Problem

A mobile phone company charges a monthly standing fee of 12 pounds plus 5 pence for every minute of calls made. Last month, a customer received a bill for 27 pounds. How many minutes of calls did they make?

Let m be the number of minutes. Since the standing fee is in pounds (12) and the rate is in pence (5), convert the rate to pounds: 5 pence is 0.05 pounds. The total bill expression is 0.05m + 12 = 27. Subtracting 12 from both sides gives 0.05m = 15. Dividing both sides by 0.05 gives m = 300. The customer made 300 minutes of calls.

An operation that reverses the effect of another operation, such as subtraction reversing addition.

A number placed in front of and multiplying a variable in an algebraic expression.

Integers that follow each other in sequence, increasing by 1 each time, such as x, x + 1, and x + 2.

اعرض الحل
  1. Define the variable: Let Tom's current age be x. Sarah's current age is 3x.
  2. Set up expressions for their ages in 4 years: Tom will be x + 4, and Sarah will be 3x + 4.
  3. Form the equation based on their sum: (x + 4) + (3x + 4) = 38.
  4. Simplify the equation: 4x + 8 = 38.
  5. Solve for x by subtracting 8: 4x = 30.
  6. Divide by 4: x = 7.5.
  7. Interpret the result: Tom is 7.5 years old, and Sarah is 3 times 7.5 = 22.5 years old.

2The length of a rectangular garden is 5 metres longer than its width. If the total perimeter is 34 metres, find its dimensions.

اعرض الحل
  1. Define the variable: Let width = w. Length = w + 5.
  2. Write the perimeter formula: Perimeter = 2 * (length + width).
  3. Form the equation: 2(w + w + 5) = 34.
  4. Simplify inside brackets: 2(2w + 5) = 34.
  5. Expand brackets: 4w + 10 = 34.
  6. Subtract 10 from both sides: 4w = 24.
  7. Divide by 4: w = 6.
  8. Find length: 6 + 5 = 11. Width is 6 metres and length is 11 metres.

مفاهيم خاطئة شائعة

خطأ شائع Learners believe that '5 less than x' translates to 5 - x.

في الحقيقة It translates to x - 5 because the quantity 5 is being removed from x, meaning x comes first in the subtraction.

خطأ شائع Learners think the variable letter chosen changes the final numerical answer.

في الحقيقة The choice of letter is arbitrary; whether using x, n, or y, the resulting equation yields the exact same numerical solution.

خطأ شائع When solving equations, learners apply inverse operations to only one side.

في الحقيقة An equation must remain balanced, so any addition, subtraction, multiplication, or division applied to one side must be applied identically to the other side.

خطأ شائع Learners stop solving once they find the value of the variable, ignoring what the question actually asked.

في الحقيقة Many problems require substituting the variable back into an expression or including specific units to provide the final answer.
