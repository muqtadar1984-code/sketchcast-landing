The explanation

Molecular Basis of Heredity: DNA, Genes, and Chromosomes

Heredity is the biological process by which traits and characteristics are transmitted from parents to their offspring. This transmission ensures that progeny resemble their ancestors while preserving individual uniqueness. The physical basis of heredity is DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), a complex nucleic acid macromolecule arranged in a double-helix structure composed of alternating sugar-phosphate backbones and nitrogenous base pairs. Found primarily within the nucleus of eukaryotic cells, DNA stores the complete genetic instructions required for the growth, development, metabolic functioning, and reproduction of living organisms.

A gene is a specific functional region of a DNA molecule that encodes instructions for synthesizing a specific polypeptide chain or functional RNA molecule. These proteins and RNA molecules ultimately determine physical and biochemical traits, such as eye colour, hair texture, and digestive enzyme production. Different alternative forms of the same gene that occupy the same locus on homologous chromosomes are known as alleles. For instance, the gene governing plant height in pea plants possesses a tall allele and a dwarf allele.

Because the total length of DNA in a human cell is exceptionally long, it undergoes tight packaging to prevent tangling and damage. DNA wraps around specialized basic proteins called histones to form nucleosomes, which then coil further into chromatin fibres and condense into distinct, thread-like structures called chromosomes during cell division. Somatic cells of human beings typically contain 46 chromosomes, organized into 23 homologous pairs. In each pair, one chromosome originates from the maternal parent through the egg cell, and the homologous counterpart originates from the paternal parent through the sperm cell. Homologous chromosomes share identical gene loci, although the specific alleles residing at those loci may differ between maternal and paternal copies, creating the genetic diversity necessary for variation.

Mendel's Contribution and Laws of Inheritance

The formal scientific study of heredity began with the pioneering breeding experiments conducted by Gregor Mendel between 1856 and 1863. Mendel chose the garden pea plant (Pisum sativum) as his experimental model because it possesses several advantageous biological characteristics: it exhibits distinct, contrasting pairs of visible traits, has a short life cycle, produces large numbers of offspring per generation, and can naturally self-pollinate or be artificially cross-pollinated.

Mendel investigated single-trait inheritance through monohybrid crosses. When he crossed a homozygous dominant parent with a homozygous recessive parent, the resulting first filial (F1) generation displayed exclusively the dominant trait. Upon allowing these F1 heterozygous individuals to self-pollinate, the second filial (F2) generation consistently yielded a phenotypic ratio of approximately three dominant individuals to one recessive individual, and a genotypic ratio of one homozygous dominant to two heterozygous to one homozygous recessive.

From these quantitative findings, Mendel formulated fundamental principles of genetics. The Law of Segregation states that allele pairs separate during gamete formation, ensuring that each gamete carries only one allele for each gene. Fertilisation then randomly restores the two-allele condition in the zygote. Additionally, Mendel formulated the Law of Independent Assortment, which dictates that alleles of different genes assort independently of one another during gamete formation, meaning the inheritance of one trait does not influence the inheritance of another, provided the genes reside on different chromosomes or are located far apart on the same chromosome.

Sex Determination in Humans

In human beings and many other diploid organisms, biological sex is determined genetically through specialized chromosomes known as sex chromosomes. Out of the 46 chromosomes found in normal human somatic cells, 22 pairs are autosomes, which regulate general somatic characteristics and show no structural dimorphism between males and females. The 23rd pair consists of the sex chromosomes, which differ significantly between genders and dictate biological sex.

Human females possess two identical large sex chromosomes designated as XX. Human males possess one large X chromosome and one significantly smaller Y chromosome designated as XY. During gamete formation through meiosis, female germ cells produce ova that all receive a single X chromosome. Conversely, male germ cells produce spermatozoa that segregate into two equal populations: half receive an X chromosome, and the other half receive a Y chromosome.

Biological sex determination depends entirely upon which type of spermatozoon successfully fertilises the ovum. If a sperm carrying an X chromosome fertilises an X-bearing ovum, the resulting zygote possesses an XX genotype and develops into a female. If a sperm carrying a Y chromosome fertilises the ovum, the resulting zygote possesses an XY genotype and develops into a male. Because the segregation of X-bearing and Y-bearing sperm occurs in equal proportions, the mathematical probability of producing a male or female child in any human pregnancy is always fifty percent.

Variation Within a Species and Its Genetic Basis

Variation refers to the observable differences in characteristics and traits that exist between individuals of the same biological species. Variation is essential for evolutionary adaptation, as it provides a diverse spectrum of traits that enable populations to survive changing environmental pressures through natural selection. Variation within a species is broadly categorized into inherited variation and acquired variation.

Inherited variation is genetic in origin and is transmitted across generations through germline cells and gametes. This form of variation is generated primarily by sexual reproduction mechanisms: the independent assortment of chromosomes during metaphase of meiosis, the physical crossing over and exchange of genetic material between non-sister chromatids during prophase I of meiosis, and the random fusion of gametes during fertilisation. Furthermore, spontaneous mutations—random alterations in the nucleotide base sequence of DNA—introduce entirely novel alleles into a population's gene pool, driving long-term evolutionary divergence.

In contrast, acquired variation develops throughout an individual organism's lifetime due to environmental influences, nutritional availability, lifestyle, or physical exercise, such as increased muscle mass resulting from heavy resistance training or skin darkening caused by solar ultraviolet radiation. Acquired variations are restricted to somatic tissues and cannot be inherited by subsequent generations because they do not alter the nucleotide sequence within the DNA of germline cells. Variation can also be classified phenotypically: continuous variation, such as human height or body mass, displays a smooth, graded range of measurements controlled by multiple interacting genes in polygenic inheritance, whereas discontinuous variation, such as human ABO blood groups or attached versus detached earlobes, falls into distinct, non-overlapping phenotypic categories controlled by single major genes.

Key terms

DNA
Deoxyribonucleic acid is a complex double-helix macromolecule that carries the complete genetic instructions for the development, functioning, and reproduction of all living organisms.
Gene
A specific functional segment of DNA that encodes instructions for synthesizing a polypeptide or functional RNA molecule, determining specific traits.
Allele
One of two or more alternative forms of a gene that occupy the same locus on homologous chromosomes.
Chromosome
A thread-like nuclear structure composed of DNA tightly coiled around histone proteins, carrying hereditary information.
Autosome
Any chromosome in a eukaryotic cell that is not a sex chromosome, controlling general somatic characteristics.
Inherited variation
Differences between individuals of a species that are genetic in origin and passed from parents to offspring through gametes.
Acquired variation
Differences in traits that develop during an individual's lifetime due to environmental influences and cannot be inherited by offspring.

Worked examples

1In pea plants, tall plant height (T) is dominant over dwarf plant height (t). If a heterozygous tall plant (Tt) is crossed with a homozygous dwarf plant (tt), predict the phenotypic and genotypic ratios of the offspring using a genetic cross.

Show the solution

Step 1: Identify the genotypes of the parents. Parent 1 is heterozygous tall (Tt). Parent 2 is homozygous dwarf (tt).

Step 2: Determine the possible gametes produced by each parent. Parent 1 produces 50% T gametes and 50% t gametes. Parent 2 produces 100% t gametes.

Step 3: Construct a Punnett square combining the gametes.

  • Top row (Parent 2): t, t
  • Left column (Parent 1): T, t
  • Cell 1 (top-left): Tt (tall)
  • Cell 2 (top-right): Tt (tall)
  • Cell 3 (bottom-left): tt (dwarf)
  • Cell 4 (bottom-right): tt (dwarf)

Step 4: Determine the genotypic ratio. There are 2 Tt and 2 tt, simplifying to a genotypic ratio of 1 Tt : 1 tt (or 50% heterozygous tall to 50% homozygous dwarf).

Step 5: Determine the phenotypic ratio. There are 2 tall plants and 2 dwarf plants, simplifying to a phenotypic ratio of 1 tall : 1 dwarf (or 50% tall to 50% dwarf).

Common misconceptions

Myth Acquired characteristics, such as muscles built through exercise or knowledge gained through study, are passed on to offspring.

In fact Acquired characteristics affect only somatic cells and do not alter the DNA sequence in germline cells, meaning they cannot be transmitted to the next generation.

Myth The mother determines the biological sex of a human child because she contributes the egg.

In fact Human biological sex is determined entirely by the type of sperm cell that fertilises the egg; sperm can carry either an X or a Y chromosome, whereas all normal human eggs carry an X chromosome.

Myth Dominant traits are always the most common traits found in a natural population.

In fact Dominance refers only to the masking of a recessive allele in a heterozygote, not to frequency; a dominant trait can be rare in a population if its allele frequency is low.

Myth Mutations are always harmful to an organism.

In fact While some mutations cause genetic disorders, many are neutral, and some provide advantageous traits that enhance survival and reproduction in changing environments.
