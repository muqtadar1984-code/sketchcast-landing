Introduction to Right-Angled Triangles and the Theorem

A right-angled triangle is a triangle in which one of the interior angles measures exactly 90 degrees. The sides of a right-angled triangle have specific names that reflect their positions relative to the right angle. The longest side is always opposite the right angle and is called the hypotenuse. The other two sides are referred to simply as the shorter sides, or the legs, of the triangle.

Pythagoras' theorem is a fundamental relationship among the three sides of a right-angled triangle. It states that the area of the square built upon the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the areas of the squares built upon the other two shorter sides. If the lengths of the two shorter sides are denoted by a and b, and the length of the hypotenuse is denoted by c, the theorem can be expressed algebraically as a2 + b2 = c2. This equation means that if the lengths of any two sides of a right-angled triangle are known, the length of the third side can be calculated.

To visualise this concept, imagine drawing a square outward from each of the three sides of a right-angled triangle. The area of the square on side a is a squared, the area of the square on side b is b squared, and the area of the square on the hypotenuse c is c squared. Pythagoras' theorem establishes that adding the numerical area of the first square to the numerical area of the second square yields the exact numerical area of the largest square on the hypotenuse.

Finding the Length of the Hypotenuse

When the lengths of the two shorter sides of a right-angled triangle are known, Pythagoras' theorem is used directly to calculate the length of the hypotenuse. Let triangle ABC have a right angle at vertex C, such that angle ACB equals 90 degrees. Let the side opposite vertex A be denoted as a, the side opposite vertex B as b, and the side opposite vertex C as c, which is the hypotenuse.

To find the length of the hypotenuse c, the squares of the two shorter sides a and b are added together. According to the formula, a2 + b2 = c2. Once the numerical value of a squared plus b squared is calculated, the square root of this sum must be taken to find the value of c. Taking the square root is a critical final step that learners frequently omit.

For example, if a shorter side measures 3 cm and the other shorter side measures 4 cm, squaring both gives 9 and 16. Adding these values together yields 25. Taking the positive square root of 25 gives 5 cm for the length of the hypotenuse. Because lengths of physical geometric figures must be positive quantities, only the positive principal square root is considered.

Finding the Length of a Shorter Side

Pythagoras' theorem can also be rearranged to find the length of one of the shorter sides when the length of the hypotenuse and the length of the other shorter side are known. Starting from the standard equation a2 + b2 = c2, isolating a squared by subtracting b squared from both sides yields a2 = c2 - b2.

In this rearranged form, the square of the known shorter side is subtracted from the square of the hypotenuse to find the square of the unknown shorter side. After performing this subtraction, the final step is to take the square root of the result to find the linear length of the side.

It is essential to subtract the shorter side squared from the hypotenuse squared, never the other way around. Because the hypotenuse is always the longest side of a right-angled triangle, its square is greater than the square of any other individual side. Subtracting in the correct order ensures that the value under the square root sign remains positive.

The Converse of Pythagoras' Theorem

The converse of Pythagoras' theorem provides a reliable method for determining whether a given triangle is a right-angled triangle when the lengths of all three sides are known. While the standard theorem starts with a right angle and deduces a relationship between the sides, the converse starts with the side lengths and deduces whether a right angle is present.

The converse states that if the square of the longest side of a triangle is equal to the sum of the squares of the other two sides, then the triangle is a right-angled triangle. Symbolically, if a triangle has side lengths a, b, and c, where c is the longest side, and a2 + b2 = c2 is true, then the angle opposite side c must be a right angle measuring 90 degrees.

To apply this test, the three side lengths are squared individually. The two smaller squared numbers are added together and compared to the square of the largest side. If the two values match perfectly, the triangle is right-angled. If the sum of the squares of the two shorter sides is less than the square of the longest side, the triangle is obtuse-angled. If the sum is greater, the triangle is acute-angled.

Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

Learners frequently encounter difficulties when applying Pythagoras' theorem due to several recurring arithmetic and conceptual errors. One common mistake is attempting to add or subtract the linear side lengths directly without squaring them first, such as calculating 3 + 4 to find a hypotenuse of 7 instead of working through the squares.

Another frequent error involves the direction of subtraction when finding a shorter side. Learners sometimes mistakenly subtract the hypotenuse from the shorter side, which results in a negative number under the square root sign and causes calculation failure. The hypotenuse must always be the starting point when calculating a missing leg because it is the largest side.

A further oversight occurs when learners correctly calculate the sum or difference of the squares but forget to take the final square root, leaving their answer expressed as an area rather than a linear length. Ensuring that every step of the calculation is checked against the geometric context helps prevent these errors.

1Calculate the length of the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle where the two shorter sides measure 6 cm and 8 cm.

تونجوقکن جاوڤن

Step 1: State Pythagoras' formula for a right-angled triangle.

Formula: a2 + b2 = c2, where c is the hypotenuse.

Step 2: Substitute the known side lengths into the formula.

Let a = 6 and let b = 8.

62 + 82 = c2

Step 3: Evaluate the squares of the two numbers.

62 = 36

82 = 64

36 + 64 = c2

Step 4: Add the squared values together.

100 = c2

Step 5: Take the positive square root of the sum to find the length of the hypotenuse.

c = square root of 100

c = 10 cm

2Calculate the length of the missing shorter side in a right-angled triangle where the hypotenuse measures 13 cm and one shorter side measures 5 cm.

تونجوقکن جاوڤن

Step 1: State Pythagoras' formula and rearrange it to solve for an unknown shorter side.

Formula: a2 + b2 = c2

Rearranged: b2 = c2 - a2, where c is the hypotenuse and a is the known shorter side.

Step 2: Substitute the known values into the rearranged formula.

Let c = 13 and let a = 5.

b2 = 132 - 52

Step 3: Evaluate the squares of the numbers.

132 = 169

52 = 25

b2 = 169 - 25

Step 4: Subtract the smaller squared value from the larger squared value.

b2 = 144

Step 5: Take the positive square root to find the length of the shorter side.

b = square root of 144

b = 12 cm
