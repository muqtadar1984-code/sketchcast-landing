Nature of Sound and Its Propagation

Sound is a form of energy that produces a sensation of hearing in our ears. It is produced by vibrating objects. When an object vibrates, it sets the surrounding particles of the medium into motion. These particles do not travel across the entire distance from the source to the listener; instead, they oscillate back and forth about their mean positions, transferring energy from one particle to the next.

As a vibrating object moves forward, it pushes and compresses the air in front of it, creating a region of high pressure and high particle density called a compression. When the object moves backward, it creates a region of low pressure and low particle density called a rarefaction. As the object continues to vibrate, a series of alternating compressions and rarefactions travels through the medium, constituting a sound wave.

Because sound waves travel by the displacement of particles in a direction parallel to the direction of wave propagation, sound is classified as a longitudinal wave. Sound can travel through various material media, including solids, liquids, and gases. The speed of sound depends on the properties of the medium, particularly its elasticity and density. In general, sound travels fastest in solids because solid particles are closely packed with strong intermolecular forces, allowing rapid energy transfer. Sound travels slower in liquids and slowest in gases.

Why Sound Needs a Medium and Speed of Sound

Unlike light or radio waves, which are electromagnetic waves and can travel through empty space, mechanical waves like sound require a material medium for propagation. A vacuum is a space devoid of matter. In a vacuum, there are no particles to vibrate, collide, and transfer mechanical energy. Therefore, sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

This principle can be demonstrated using an electric bell placed inside an airtight glass bell jar connected to a vacuum pump. When the circuit is closed, the bell rings, and the sound is clearly heard through the glass and air. As the vacuum pump removes air from the jar, the sound of the bell becomes fainter and eventually disappears, even though the hammer can still be seen striking the gong. When air is readmitted into the jar, the sound is heard once again.

The speed of sound is the distance that a sound wave travels per unit of time. In dry air at twenty degrees Celsius, the speed of sound is approximately three hundred forty-three metres per second. The speed varies with temperature; as the temperature of a medium increases, the speed of sound increases because the faster-moving particles transfer energy more rapidly. The relationship between speed, distance, and time for a sound wave is expressed mathematically as distance equals speed multiplied by time.

Waveforms, Amplitude, Loudness, Frequency, and Pitch

A sound wave can be represented graphically as a waveform, plotting displacement or pressure against distance or time. Key characteristics of a waveform include amplitude, frequency, wavelength, and time period.

Amplitude is the maximum displacement of the particles of the medium from their mean position. The loudness or softness of a sound is determined by its amplitude. A larger amplitude indicates a louder sound because more energy is being transported by the wave, causing particles to displace further. Loudness is measured in decibels.

Frequency is the number of complete oscillations or cycles per second, measured in hertz. The pitch of a sound is directly determined by its frequency. A high-frequency sound produces a high-pitched sound, such as the chirp of a bird or the squeak of a mouse. A low-frequency sound produces a low-pitched sound, such as the roar of a lion or the beat of a drum. The time period is the time taken for one complete oscillation, which is the reciprocal of frequency.

Wavelength is the distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions. The speed of a wave is equal to the product of its frequency and its wavelength.

Waveform Superposition, Reinforcement, and Cancellation

When two or more sound waves travel through the same medium simultaneously, they pass through each other and interact according to the principle of superposition. At any point where the waves meet, the resultant displacement is the vector sum of the displacements of the individual waves.

If the crest of one wave meets the crest of another wave, or a compression meets a compression, the displacements are in the same direction. The waves reinforce each other, creating a wave with a larger amplitude than either individual wave. This phenomenon is known as constructive interference or reinforcement, resulting in a louder sound at that location.

Conversely, if the crest of one wave meets the trough of another wave, or a compression meets a rarefaction, the displacements are in opposite directions. The waves cancel each other out partially or completely, creating a wave with a smaller amplitude. This phenomenon is known as destructive interference or cancellation, resulting in a softer sound or complete silence at that location. This wave interaction explains acoustic phenomena such as beats and dead spots in auditoriums.

Reflection of Sound, Echoes, and Ultrasound

Sound waves obey the laws of reflection, bouncing off hard surfaces just like light. An echo is a distinct sound heard after the reflection of sound waves from a rigid obstacle such as a cliff, large building, or wall. Because the human brain retains a sound sensation for one-tenth of a second, the reflected sound must reach the ear at least zero point one seconds after the original sound for an echo to be distinguished from the direct sound.

Using the minimum time interval and the speed of sound in air, the minimum distance required from a reflecting surface to hear an echo can be calculated. If the speed of sound is three hundred forty-three metres per second, the total distance travelled by the sound in zero point one seconds is thirty-four point three metres. Dividing by two gives a minimum distance of seventeen point one metres between the sound source and the obstacle.

The human ear can typically hear sounds with frequencies ranging from twenty hertz to twenty thousand hertz, known as the audible range. Sounds with frequencies below twenty hertz are called infrasound, while frequencies above twenty thousand hertz are called ultrasound. Ultrasound has numerous applications due to its high frequency and directional stability, including medical imaging, industrial flaw detection, and sonar for measuring ocean depths and locating underwater objects.

ايستيله اوتام

Sound wave
A longitudinal mechanical wave consisting of alternating compressions and rarefactions that propagates through a material medium.
Compression
A region in a longitudinal wave where particles are pushed together, resulting in high pressure and high density.
Rarefaction
A region in a longitudinal wave where particles are spread apart, resulting in low pressure and low density.
Amplitude
The maximum displacement of particles from their mean position in a wave.
Frequency
The number of complete wave cycles produced per second, measured in hertz.
Pitch
The characteristic of sound that depends on frequency, determining whether a sound is high or low.
Loudness
The characteristic of sound that depends on amplitude, determining how loud or soft a sound is perceived.
Echo
The repetition of a sound caused by the reflection of sound waves from a hard surface.
Ultrasound
Sound waves with frequencies above the upper limit of human hearing, specifically greater than twenty thousand hertz.
Superposition
The principle that when two or more waves meet, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements.

چونتوه برجاوڤن

1A person claps their hands near a tall cliff and hears the echo after two seconds. If the speed of sound in air is 343 metres per second, calculate the distance between the person and the cliff.

تونجوقکن جاوڤن
  1. Identify the given values: time taken for the echo to be heard (t = 2 seconds), speed of sound (v = 343 m/s).
  2. Understand that the sound travels to the cliff and back to the person, covering twice the distance (2d) between the person and the cliff.
  3. Use the formula for distance: Total distance = speed multiplied by time.
  4. Calculate total distance: 343 m/s multiplied by 2 s = 686 metres.
  5. Divide the total distance by 2 to find the one-way distance to the cliff: 686 m / 2 = 343 metres.
  6. The distance between the person and the cliff is 343 metres.

ساله فهم بياسا

ميتوس Sound waves can travel through the vacuum of space.

سبنرڽ Sound is a mechanical wave that requires a material medium of particles to oscillate and transfer energy; therefore, sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

ميتوس Loud sound travels faster than soft sound.

سبنرڽ The speed of sound depends on the properties of the medium and temperature, not on the amplitude or loudness of the sound wave.

ميتوس High pitch means the sound is louder.

سبنرڽ Pitch is determined by frequency, whereas loudness is determined by amplitude. A sound can be high-pitched and soft, or low-pitched and loud.

ميتوس Particles of the medium travel all the way from the sound source to the listener's ear.

سبنرڽ Particles only oscillate back and forth about their mean positions, transferring energy through the medium without travelling the entire distance themselves.
