Properties of Metals and Non-Metals

Elements are classified fundamentally into metals and non-metals based on their physical and chemical properties. Metals generally exhibit high thermal and electrical conductivity, are malleable (can be hammered into thin sheets), ductile (can be drawn into wires), and possess a characteristic lustre. They are typically solid at room temperature, with the exception of mercury, which is a liquid. Chemically, metal atoms have relatively few valence electrons (usually one to three) and tend to lose these outer electrons during chemical reactions to form positively charged ions known as cations.

Non-metals, found predominantly on the right side of the periodic table, show great variability in their physical states. They can be solids, liquids, or gases at room temperature. Solid non-metals are brittle and lack lustre, and they are generally poor conductors of heat and electricity, with the notable exception of graphite. Chemically, non-metal atoms have four to eight valence electrons and tend to gain or share electrons during chemical reactions to achieve a stable octet configuration.

When metals react with oxygen, they form basic oxides. For instance, magnesium burns in air with a dazzling white flame to produce white magnesium oxide powder. When this oxide is dissolved in water, it forms magnesium hydroxide, which turns red litmus blue, demonstrating its alkaline nature. Conversely, non-metals react with oxygen to form acidic or neutral oxides. For example, burning sulfur in air produces sulfur dioxide, which dissolves in water to form sulfurous acid, turning blue litmus red.

Reactions of metals with water and acids further highlight their metallic character. Highly reactive metals like sodium and potassium react violently with cold water to form metal hydroxides and hydrogen gas. Less reactive metals, such as iron and zinc, do not react with cold water but react slowly with steam to form metal oxides and hydrogen gas. Metals that are more reactive than hydrogen will displace hydrogen from dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, producing a metal salt and hydrogen gas.

Reactivity Series of Metals

The reactivity series is an arrangement of metals in order of descending chemical reactivity. This vertical listing allows scientists to predict how a metal will behave in various chemical environments, particularly in displacement reactions and interactions with oxygen, water, and acids. The standard series, spanning from most reactive to least reactive, includes potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, lead, hydrogen, copper, mercury, silver, and gold.

At the very top of the series are the highly reactive alkali metals, potassium and sodium, followed by alkaline earth metals like calcium and magnesium. These metals react vigorously with cold water and dilute acids, and they tarnish and oxidize rapidly in air. Because of their extreme reactivity, they are never found in their elemental state in nature and must be stored under oil to prevent contact with moisture and oxygen.

Moving down the series, aluminium, zinc, and iron display moderate reactivity. Aluminium forms a protective oxide layer on its surface that prevents further rapid corrosion. Zinc and iron react slowly with dilute acids and steam, and they corrode over extended periods. Towards the bottom of the series are copper, mercury, silver, and gold. These metals have very low reactivity. Copper and mercury do not react with dilute acids at all, while silver and gold are noble metals that do not react with oxygen, water, or acids under normal conditions, which explains why they retain their shiny appearance indefinitely and are frequently found uncombined in nature.

Hydrogen is included in the reactivity series as a reference point. Metals situated above hydrogen in the series can displace hydrogen from dilute acids, whereas metals situated below hydrogen cannot.

Displacement Reactions

A displacement reaction occurs when a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt. These reactions are classic examples of oxidation-reduction (redox) processes, where the more reactive metal loses electrons (undergoes oxidation) and the less reactive metal ions gain electrons (undergo reduction).

To predict whether a displacement reaction will occur, one consults the reactivity series. If a solid metal is placed into an aqueous solution containing ions of another metal that sits lower in the reactivity series, a reaction will take place. For example, if a piece of polished zinc metal is immersed in a blue aqueous solution of copper(II) sulfate, a spontaneous reaction occurs. Over time, the blue colour of the solution fades and eventually becomes colourless, while a reddish-brown coating of copper metal deposits on the zinc strip. This happens because zinc is more reactive than copper; the zinc atoms lose electrons to become zinc ions in solution, while the copper ions in the solution accept those electrons to form solid copper atoms.

Conversely, if a piece of copper metal is placed into a colourless solution of zinc sulfate, no reaction is observed. Copper is lower in the reactivity series than zinc and therefore lacks the chemical driving force to displace zinc ions from the solution. Similarly, placing silver into copper sulfate solution yields no reaction because silver is less reactive than copper.

Formation and Properties of Ionic Compounds

The reactivity of metals is fundamentally linked to their tendency to lose valence electrons to achieve a stable, noble gas electron configuration. When a metal atom reacts with a non-metal atom, a complete transfer of electrons takes place. The metal atom loses one or more electrons to form a positively charged cation, while the non-metal atom gains those electrons to form a negatively charged anion. The electrostatic attraction between these oppositely charged ions constitutes an ionic bond, resulting in the formation of an ionic compound.

A classic example is the formation of sodium chloride. A sodium atom transfers its single valence electron to a chlorine atom, which has seven valence electrons. The resulting sodium cation and chloride anion are held together in a vast three-dimensional crystal lattice structure.

Ionic compounds exhibit distinct physical properties arising from the strong electrostatic forces holding their ions together:

  • Physical State: They are solid at room temperature and exist as hard, crystalline structures due to the strong inter-ionic forces.
  • Melting and Boiling Points: They have high melting and boiling points because a large amount of thermal energy is required to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions between the ions in the crystal lattice.
  • Solubility: Most ionic compounds are soluble in polar solvents like water, as water molecules interact with and stabilize the individual ions.
  • Electrical Conductivity: Ionic compounds do not conduct electricity in the solid state because their ions are locked into fixed positions within the crystal lattice. However, when melted (in the molten state) or dissolved in water, the ions become mobile and are free to carry an electrical charge, making the liquid or solution a good conductor.

Basic Metallurgical Processes

Metallurgy refers to the step-by-step scientific and industrial process used for extracting pure metals from their naturally occurring ores. An ore is a mineral deposit from which a metal can be profitably extracted. The metallurgical process generally involves three main stages: concentration of the ore, extraction of the metal, and refining of the crude metal.

Before extraction, the mined ore contains unwanted earthly impurities collectively known as gangue. The concentration step removes these impurities through physical or chemical methods, such as hydraulic washing, froth flotation, or magnetic separation.

Once concentrated, the method used to extract the metal depends heavily on its position in the reactivity series:

  • Extraction of Highly Reactive Metals: Metals at the top of the reactivity series, such as sodium, magnesium, and aluminium, have a very high affinity for oxygen and cannot be reduced by conventional carbon reduction agents. Instead, they are extracted by electrolytic reduction of their molten chlorides or oxides. During electrolysis, metal ions migrate to the cathode, where they gain electrons to form the pure liquid metal.
  • Extraction of Moderately Reactive Metals: Metals in the middle of the reactivity series, such as zinc, iron, and lead, usually occur as sulfides or carbonates. Sulfide ores are converted into oxides by roasting (heating strongly in the presence of excess air), while carbonate ores are converted into oxides by calcination (heating strongly in a limited supply of air). These metal oxides are subsequently reduced to the metallic state by heating them with a reducing agent such as carbon (coke) in a blast furnace.
  • Extraction of Least Reactive Metals: Metals at the bottom of the reactivity series, such as mercury and copper, occur as sulfides and can sometimes be extracted simply by heating their ores in air, which decomposes the sulfide directly to the metal.

The crude metal obtained from the extraction stage often contains impurities. Refining is the final purification process, with electrorefining being the most widely used method. In electrorefining, an impure block of the metal is used as the anode, a pure strip of the metal is used as the cathode, and an aqueous salt solution of the metal serves as the electrolyte.

Corrosion and Its Prevention

Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of metals through chemical or electrochemical reactions with their surrounding environment. The most familiar example of corrosion is the rusting of iron, which occurs when iron is exposed to both oxygen and moisture over an extended period. Chemically, rusting involves the oxidation of iron to form hydrated iron(III) oxide, a flaky reddish-brown substance that does not adhere tightly to the underlying metal, allowing the corrosion process to continue deeper into the structure.

Other metals also undergo surface oxidation, but with different results. When aluminium corrodes, it forms a thin, dense, unreactive layer of aluminium oxide on its surface. This oxide layer adheres firmly and acts as a protective barrier, preventing further internal corrosion. Copper corrodes in damp air to form a green coating consisting of a mixture of copper carbonate and copper hydroxide, commonly known as patina. Silver tarnishes when it reacts with trace sulfur compounds in the air, forming a dark layer of silver sulfide.

Because corrosion causes massive economic loss and structural degradation, various prevention methods are employed to protect metals:

  • Barrier Protection: Applying a physical barrier between the metal surface and atmospheric moisture and oxygen. Common methods include painting, oiling, greasing, or coating with plastic.
  • Galvanising: Coating iron or steel objects with a thin layer of zinc. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so even if the coating is scratched, the zinc corrodes preferentially, sacrificing itself to protect the underlying iron.
  • Electroplating: Depositing a layer of a corrosion-resistant metal, such as chromium or nickel, onto the surface of another metal using an electric current.
  • Sacrificial Protection: Attaching blocks of a more reactive metal, such as magnesium or zinc, to steel structures like ship hulls or underground pipelines. The more reactive metal corrodes instead of the protected structure.
  • Alloying: Melting a metal together with one or more other metals or non-metals to change its properties. For example, mixing iron with chromium and nickel produces stainless steel, which does not rust.

Metals
Elements that are generally shiny, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat and electricity, and tend to form positive ions by losing electrons.
Non-metals
Elements that are typically brittle, poor conductors of heat and electricity, and tend to gain or share electrons in chemical reactions.
Reactivity series
A list of metals arranged in order of their decreasing chemical reactivity, used to predict displacement and chemical behaviour.
Displacement reaction
A chemical reaction in which a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from an aqueous solution of its salt.
Cation
A positively charged ion formed when a neutral atom loses one or more electrons.
Anion
A negatively charged ion formed when a neutral atom gains one or more electrons.
Ionic bond
The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in an ionic compound.
Ore
A naturally occurring mineral deposit from which a metal can be extracted economically and profitably.
Metallurgy
The scientific and industrial process of extracting pure metals from their ores and refining them for use.
Corrosion
The gradual destruction or deterioration of metals by chemical or electrochemical reaction with their environment.

Step 1: Consult the reactivity series for the relative positions of the metals involved.

  • For Test Tube 1: Copper and iron. In the reactivity series, iron is placed above copper, meaning iron is more reactive than copper.
  • For Test Tube 2: Zinc and copper. In the reactivity series, zinc is placed above copper, meaning zinc is more reactive than copper.

Step 2: Apply the rule of displacement reactions. A displacement reaction occurs only when a more reactive metal is added to a solution containing ions of a less reactive metal.

  • Test Tube 1: Copper is less reactive than iron. Therefore, copper cannot displace iron from iron(II) sulfate. No reaction occurs.
  • Test Tube 2: Zinc is more reactive than copper. Therefore, zinc will displace copper from copper(II) sulfate. A reaction occurs.

ساله فهم بياسا

ميتوس All metals react vigorously with cold water.

سبنرڽ Only very reactive metals like potassium and sodium react vigorously with cold water. Many metals, such as magnesium, react only with steam, while iron and copper do not react with water at room temperature.

ميتوس A less reactive metal can displace a more reactive metal from its salt solution if the solution is heated.

سبنرڽ Displacement reactions are dictated by thermodynamics and the reactivity series; heating cannot force a less reactive metal to displace a more reactive one.

ميتوس Ionic compounds conduct electricity in their solid state because they contain charged ions.

سبنرڽ While ionic compounds contain charged ions, those ions are locked rigidly in place within the crystal lattice and cannot move; ionic compounds conduct electricity only when molten or dissolved in water.

ميتوس Rusting requires only oxygen to occur.

سبنرڽ Rusting requires both oxygen and moisture (water) simultaneously. Iron will not rust in dry air or in oxygen-free water.
