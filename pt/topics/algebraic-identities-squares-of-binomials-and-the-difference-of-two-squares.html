A explicação

Introduction to Algebraic Identities

An algebraic identity is an equality that holds true for all possible values of the variables involved. Unlike a conditional equation, which can be solved to find specific values of an unknown variable, an identity is a statement of mathematical equivalence. The word identity comes from the fact that both sides of the equation are identically equal, regardless of what numbers are substituted into the letters. This property makes identities exceptionally powerful tools in algebra. They allow mathematicians and scientists to transform complex expressions into simpler, more manageable forms without changing their underlying value.

In early algebra, expressions involving brackets are often expanded using the distributive law, multiplying each term in the first bracket by each term in the second bracket. For binomials, which are expressions consisting of two terms separated by a plus or minus sign, this process follows the FOIL method, standing for First, Outer, Inner, and Last. When multiplying identical binomials, or binomials that differ only in the sign between their terms, certain structural patterns emerge repeatedly. Rather than performing the full multiplication every single time, mathematicians formalise these repeating patterns into standard algebraic identities.

Mastering these identities saves time and reduces the likelihood of calculation errors during manipulation. The three most fundamental identities taught at the lower-secondary level involve squaring binomials and taking the product of a sum and a difference. Understanding where these formulas come from requires connecting algebra to geometry, visualising areas of rectangles and squares, and applying the standard rules of arithmetic and signed numbers. The following sections examine each of these three core identities in detail, explaining their derivation, their algebraic mechanics, their geometric proof, and their practical application in both expansion and factorisation.

The Square of a Sum Identity

The square of a sum identity deals with expressions of the form (a + b)2, where a and b represent any numbers, variables, or algebraic terms. To understand where the formula comes from, one can write the squared bracket as the product of two identical brackets: (a + b)(a + b). Applying the distributive law, every term in the first bracket multiplies every term in the second bracket. First, multiply a by a to get a2. Next, multiply a by b to get ab. Then, multiply b by a to get ba, which is algebraically identical to ab. Finally, multiply b by b to get b2. Combining these individual products gives a2 + ab + ab + b2. Collecting the two like terms, ab and ab, yields the final standard identity: (a + b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2.

This expansion can also be visualised geometrically using area. Imagine a large square whose side length is divided into two parts, a and b. The total area of this square is (a + b)2. Inside the large square, the grid lines divide the shape into four smaller regions: a square of area a2, a rectangle of area ab, another rectangle of area ab, and a smaller square of area b2. Adding the areas of all four individual regions together gives the exact same result: a2 + 2ab + b2. This geometric interpretation provides concrete proof that the algebraic formula is correct.

When applying this identity to numerical or algebraic expressions, the first step is to correctly identify the terms playing the roles of a and b. For example, in the expression (x + 5)2, the variable x acts as a, and the number 5 acts as b. Substituting these into the formula yields x2 plus two times x times 5 plus 52. Simplifying this produces x2 + 10x + 25. The middle term, 2ab, is often called the cross term because it results from multiplying the two different terms inside the bracket together and doubling the result. Failing to include this middle term is one of the most frequent errors made by learners.

The Square of a Difference Identity

The square of a difference identity applies to expressions of the form (a - b)2. Following the same foundational method as the square of a sum, this expression can be written out as the product of two identical binomials: (a - b)(a - b). Expanding this product using the distributive law requires careful attention to negative and positive signs. First, multiply a by a to obtain a2. Next, multiply a by negative b to obtain negative ab. Then, multiply negative b by a to obtain another negative ab. Finally, multiply negative b by negative b, which yields a positive b2 because a negative multiplied by a negative gives a positive. Combining all the terms yields a2 minus ab minus ab plus b2. Collecting the like terms, negative ab and negative ab, combines to give negative 2ab. Thus, the identity is established: (a - b)2 = a2 - 2ab + b2.

Comparing this identity to the square of a sum, the only difference is the sign of the middle term. In the square of a sum, the middle term is positive 2ab. In the square of a difference, the middle term is negative 2ab. The final term, b2, remains positive in both identities because squaring any non-zero real number, whether positive or negative, always results in a positive value. Learners frequently make the mistake of making the final term negative, believing that the subtraction sign inside the bracket carries through to the end of the expression.

Applying this identity follows the exact same substitution procedure as the sum identity. For instance, consider the expression (3x - 4)2. Here, a is 3x and b is 4. Substituting these values into the formula gives (3x)2 minus two times (3x) times 4 plus 42. Squaring the first term, 3x, requires squaring both the coefficient and the variable, resulting in 9x2. The middle term calculates as two times 3x times 4, which is 24x. The final term is 4 squared, which is 16. Putting it all together gives 9x2 - 24x + 16. This structured approach eliminates the need to write out the full multiplication and reduces errors with negative numbers.

The Difference of Two Squares Identity

The third major algebraic identity is the difference of two squares, represented by the formula a2 - b2 = (a + b)(a - b). Unlike the previous two identities, which deal with squaring a complete binomial, this identity describes the product of two specific binomials: one being the sum of two terms, and the other being the difference of the exact same two terms. Expanding the right-hand side, (a + b)(a - b), using the distributive law demonstrates why the formula works. Multiply a by a to get a2. Multiply a by negative b to get negative ab. Multiply b by a to get positive ab. Multiply b by negative b to get negative b2. Writing these out gives a2 minus ab plus ab minus b2. Notice that the two middle terms, negative ab and positive ab, are exact opposites. They cancel each other out completely, leaving only a2 minus b2.

This cancellation of the middle terms is the defining feature of the difference of two squares. It occurs whenever multiplying two binomials that share identical first terms and identical second terms, but differ in the arithmetic sign connecting them. The geometric proof of this identity involves taking a square of side length a, cutting out a smaller square of side length b from one corner, and rearranging the remaining L-shaped region into a rectangle with dimensions (a + b) and (a - b). The area remains constant, proving that a2 minus b2 equals the product of (a + b) and (a - b).

This identity operates in two directions: expansion and factorisation. When expanding, it converts a product of brackets into a binomial with squared terms. When factorising, it takes a binomial consisting of two squared terms separated by a subtraction sign and breaks it down into two brackets. For example, the expression x2 - 49 can be factorised immediately by recognising that x2 is the square of x, and 49 is the square of 7. Applying the identity yields (x + 7)(x - 7). This technique is essential for simplifying algebraic fractions and solving quadratic equations.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Algebraic identities are powerful, but learners frequently commit specific conceptual errors when first encountering them. Identifying these pitfalls helps build robust mathematical habits and prevents persistent misconceptions. The single most common error is known informally as the freshman's dream. This occurs when a learner incorrectly assumes that the square of a sum distributes directly over the addition, writing (a + b)2 as a2 + b2 and completely omitting the middle cross term, 2ab. To avoid this error, always remember that squaring a binomial means multiplying the entire bracket by itself, which necessitates the full distribution of terms, resulting in three distinct terms rather than two.

A second frequent mistake involves sign errors when expanding the square of a difference, (a - b)2. Learners often write the final term as negative b2, incorrectly assuming that the negative sign inside the bracket applies to the final squared term. The correction relies on the fundamental rule of arithmetic that squaring any real number always produces a non-negative result. Because negative b multiplied by negative b equals positive b2, the final term in a squared binomial is always positive, regardless of whether a sum or a difference is being squared.

A third error occurs with the difference of two squares, where learners attempt to apply the identity when the two terms are separated by a plus sign, writing a2 + b2 as (a + b)(a + b). This is false; the sum of two squares, a2 + b2, cannot be factorised further using real numbers and is not equal to (a + b)2. The identity requires a subtraction sign between the two squared terms. Finally, when applying the identities to terms with coefficients, such as (2x + 3)2, learners often forget to square the numerical coefficient, writing 2x2 instead of 4x2. Every component inside the term must be squared entirely.

Exemplos resolvidos

1Expand and simplify the algebraic expression (2x + 3)2 using the square of a sum identity.

Mostrar a solução

Step 1: Identify the formula for the square of a sum: (a + b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2.

Step 2: Assign the terms from the problem to a and b. Here, a = 2x and b = 3.

Step 3: Substitute these values into the identity formula: (2x + 3)2 = (2x)2 + 2(2x)(3) + (3)2.

Step 4: Expand each part of the expression. Squaring 2x gives 4x2. Multiplying 2 times 2x times 3 gives 12x. Squaring 3 gives 9.

Step 5: Write the final simplified expression: 4x2 + 12x + 9.

2Expand and simplify the expression (5p - 2q)2 using the square of a difference identity.

Mostrar a solução

Step 1: Identify the formula for the square of a difference: (a - b)2 = a2 - 2ab + b2.

Step 2: Assign the terms from the problem to a and b. Here, a = 5p and b = 2q.

Step 3: Substitute these values into the identity formula: (5p - 2q)2 = (5p)2 - 2(5p)(2q) + (2q)2.

Step 4: Expand each part. Squaring 5p gives 25p2. Multiplying 2 times 5p times 2q gives 20pq. Squaring 2q gives 4q2.

Step 5: Write the final simplified expression: 25p2 - 20pq + 4q2.

Ideias erradas comuns

Ideia errada Believing that (a + b)2 is equal to a2 + b2 by distributing the exponent across the addition.

Na verdade The square of a sum always includes the middle cross term, 2ab, because expanding (a + b)(a + b) produces four individual products that combine into a2 + 2ab + b2.

Ideia errada Believing that the final term in the square of a difference, (a - b)2, is negative b2.

Na verdade The final term is always positive b2 because squaring any non-zero number, whether positive or negative, results in a positive value since negative b multiplied by negative b is positive b2.

Ideia errada Assuming that the sum of two squares, a2 + b2, can be factorised into (a + b)(a - b).

Na verdade The difference of two squares identity requires a subtraction sign between the terms; the sum of two squares cannot be factorised into linear binomials using real numbers.

Ideia errada Squaring only the variable when expanding a term with a coefficient, such as writing (3x)2 as 3x2.

Na verdade Both the numerical coefficient and the variable must be squared entirely, so (3x)2 becomes 32 times x2, which equals 9x2.
