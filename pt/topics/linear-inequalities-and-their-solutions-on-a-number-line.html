A explicação

Introduction to Linear Inequalities

An equation expresses a strict balance between two mathematical expressions, such as 3x + 2 = 11, which yields a single specific value for the unknown variable x. However, many real-world situations involve limits and ranges rather than fixed quantities. A linear inequality compares two expressions where one is strictly greater than, greater than or equal to, less than, or less than or equal to the other. The term linear indicates that the highest power of the variable is one, meaning no exponents higher than one appear in the expressions.

Four fundamental symbols are used to write linear inequalities. The symbol less than (<) indicates that the quantity on the left is smaller than the quantity on the right. The symbol greater than (>) indicates that the left quantity is larger. When equality is included as a possibility, the symbols less than or equal to (<=) and greater than or equal to (>=) are used. Understanding these symbols is the foundation for interpreting algebraic constraints.

Consider a simple physical scenario, such as a ride at an amusement park that requires a person to be at least 140 centimetres tall. If height is represented by the variable h, this restriction is written as the inequality h >= 140. Unlike an equation with one exact answer, this inequality has an infinite number of solutions. Any height of 140 centimetres, 141.5 centimetres, 150 centimetres, or 200 centimetres satisfies the condition. When working with inequalities, finding the solution means determining the entire set of numbers that make the statement true, rather than stopping at a single isolated value.

Solving Linear Inequalities Using Balance Methods

Solving a linear inequality follows the same logical steps used to solve a standard linear equation. The goal is to isolate the variable on one side of the inequality symbol by performing inverse operations. Whatever operation is performed on one side of the inequality must also be performed on the other side to maintain the relative order of the expressions.

To solve an inequality like x + 5 > 12, the inverse operation of adding five is subtracting five from both sides. Subtracting five from x + 5 leaves x, and subtracting five from 12 leaves seven. This produces the solution x > 7. Similarly, for an inequality involving multiplication such as 4x <= 24, dividing both sides by four isolates the variable, resulting in x <= 6.

Multi-step inequalities require a specific order of operations, typically undoing additions or subtractions before multiplications or divisions. For example, to solve 3x - 4 < 11, the first step is to add four to both sides, yielding 3x < 15. Then, dividing both sides by three gives x < 5. Throughout these algebraic manipulations, the inequality symbol remains unchanged because all operations involve positive numbers or simple additions and subtractions. The algebraic balance is preserved at every stage, ensuring that the solution set accurately reflects the original constraint.

The Negative Multiplier Rule

A crucial rule distinguishes the manipulation of inequalities from the manipulation of equations. When multiplying or dividing both sides of an inequality by a negative number, the inequality sign must be reversed. This means less than becomes greater than, greater than becomes less than, less than or equal to becomes greater than or equal to, and greater than or equal to becomes less than or equal to.

To understand why this rule is necessary, consider a simple numerical statement that is clearly true, such as 2 < 5. If both sides of this true statement are multiplied by a positive number, such as three, the result is 6 < 15, which remains true. However, if both sides are multiplied by a negative number, such as negative three, the left side becomes negative six and the right side becomes negative fifteen. On the number line, negative fifteen is further to the left than negative six, meaning negative fifteen is smaller than negative six. Therefore, the statement -6 < -15 is false. To make the mathematical relationship correct, the inequality symbol must be flipped, resulting in -6 > -15.

In algebraic problem-solving, failing to reverse the inequality sign when dividing by a negative coefficient is a frequent error. For instance, consider -2x < 10. To isolate x, both sides must be divided by negative two. Performing this division while remembering the reversal rule changes the less than sign to a greater than sign, yielding x > -5. The negative sign attached to the variable coefficient dictates this mandatory reversal, regardless of whether the constant on the other side of the inequality is positive or negative.

Representing Solution Sets on a Number Line

An algebraic solution such as x > 3 or x <= -2 communicates the boundary of a range, but representing this solution visually on a number line provides immediate clarity about the infinite set of numbers included. A standard horizontal number line is used, marked with integers, and specific graphical conventions indicate whether boundary points are included in the solution set.

An open circle plotted on a number line indicates that the boundary value itself is not included in the solution set. This graphical representation corresponds directly to strict inequality symbols, which are less than (<) and greater than (>). For example, the solution x > 3 is drawn by placing an open circle at the number three and drawing a bold arrow extending infinitely to the right, showing that all numbers greater than three satisfy the condition.

A closed or shaded circle indicates that the boundary value is included in the solution set. This representation corresponds to non-strict inequality symbols, which are less than or equal to (<=) and greater than or equal to (>=). For instance, the solution x <= -2 is drawn by placing a solid, filled circle at negative two and drawing a bold arrow extending infinitely to the left, showing that negative two and all numbers smaller than negative two are valid solutions.

Compound inequalities, which bound a variable between two numbers such as -1 < x <= 4, combine these conventions. Two circles are placed on the number line, one open at negative one and one closed at four, with a bold line segment connecting them to show that the solution lies in the interval between those two values.

Worked Examples of Linear Inequalities

Working through complete examples demonstrates how algebraic rules and graphical representations integrate into a single cohesive process. The first example involves a standard multi-step inequality with positive coefficients. Consider solving and graphing 5x + 3 >= 23. Subtracting three from both sides gives 5x >= 20. Dividing both sides by five yields x >= 4. On the number line, this is represented by a solid closed circle at four and an arrow pointing to the right.

The second example incorporates the negative multiplier rule. Consider solving and graphing 7 - 3x < 16. First, subtract seven from both sides to isolate the variable term, resulting in -3x < 9. Next, divide both sides by negative three. Because the division involves a negative number, the less than symbol must be reversed to a greater than symbol. This produces the final solution x > -3. On the number line, this is represented by an open circle at negative three and an arrow pointing to the right.

The third example features variables on both sides of the inequality. Consider solving 4x - 5 <= 2x + 7. To collect the variable terms on the left, subtract 2x from both sides, yielding 2x - 5 <= 7. Adding five to both sides gives 2x <= 12. Finally, dividing by two produces x <= 6. On the number line, this is represented by a closed circle at six and an arrow pointing to the left, capturing all numbers equal to or smaller than six.

Common Misconceptions and How to Avoid Them

Learners frequently encounter specific conceptual hurdles when studying linear inequalities, often driven by the temptation to treat inequalities exactly like equations. Recognizing these pitfalls helps establish reliable problem-solving habits. One major misconception is believing that the inequality sign should be reversed whenever a negative number appears anywhere in the problem. In reality, the sign is only reversed when the specific operation of multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number occurs. The presence of a negative constant or a negative number being subtracted does not trigger a sign reversal.

Another common error involves confusing open and closed circles on the number line. Learners sometimes place solid dots for strict inequalities like x < 4 or open circles for inclusive inequalities like x >= -1. Remembering that equality requires a solid shape helps: the extra ink used to draw the underline in the symbol (<= or >=) matches the extra ink used to fill in the circle.

A third error occurs when learners transpose the variable to the right side and read the inequality backward. For example, solving an inequality and arriving at 5 > x can confuse students who are accustomed to seeing the variable on the left. Translating this statement to read from the variable's perspective, so that x < 5, clarifies the direction of the arrow needed when drawing the solution on the number line.

Termos-chave

Linear Inequality
A mathematical statement comparing two linear expressions using an inequality symbol, having an infinite range of solutions.
Open Circle
A graphical symbol used on a number line to show that a boundary value is not included in the solution set for strict inequalities.
Closed Circle
A graphical symbol used on a number line to show that a boundary value is included in the solution set for inclusive inequalities.
Negative Multiplier Rule
The mathematical rule stating that the inequality sign must be reversed whenever both sides are multiplied or divided by a negative number.
Solution Set
The complete collection of all numbers that satisfy a given inequality or equation.

Exemplos resolvidos

1Solve and graph the inequality 5x + 3 >= 23.

Mostrar a solução

Step 1: Write down the original inequality: 5x + 3 >= 23.

Step 2: Subtract 3 from both sides to isolate the variable term: 5x >= 23 - 3, which simplifies to 5x >= 20.

Step 3: Divide both sides by the positive coefficient 5: x >= 20 / 5.

Step 4: Calculate the final inequality: x >= 4.

Step 5: Represent the solution on a number line by placing a closed circle at 4 and drawing a bold arrow extending to the right.

Number line: x ≥ 4 — a filled circle at 4 and an arrow to the right.

2Solve and graph the inequality 7 - 3x < 16.

Mostrar a solução

Step 1: Write down the original inequality: 7 - 3x < 16.

Step 2: Subtract 7 from both sides to isolate the variable term: -3x < 16 - 7, which simplifies to -3x < 9.

Step 3: Divide both sides by the negative coefficient -3. Because this is a division by a negative number, reverse the inequality sign from < to >.

Step 4: Calculate the final inequality: x > -3.

Step 5: Represent the solution on a number line by placing an open circle at -3 and drawing a bold arrow extending to the right.

Number line: x > -3 — a open circle at -3 and an arrow to the right.

Ideias erradas comuns

Ideia errada The inequality sign must always be flipped whenever a negative number appears in the problem.

Na verdade The sign is only reversed when multiplying or dividing both sides of the inequality by a negative number, not simply because a negative number is present.

Ideia errada Open and closed circles on the number line can be used interchangeably for any inequality.

Na verdade Open circles are reserved for strict inequalities where the boundary is excluded, while closed circles are used for inclusive inequalities where the boundary is included.

Ideia errada Inequalities have a single isolated solution value just like linear equations.

Na verdade Linear inequalities describe a continuous range or interval of numbers, yielding an infinite set of possible solutions.
