Penerangan

The Cell: Basic Unit of Life

The cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all known living organisms. All living things, from the smallest bacteria to the largest whales, are composed of one or more cells. Organisms can be broadly categorised based on the number of cells they possess. Unicellular organisms consist of a single cell, which carries out all life processes. Examples include bacteria and amoebae. Multicellular organisms, on the other hand, are composed of many cells working together. Humans, animals, and plants are all multicellular organisms.

Cells are incredibly diverse in size, shape, and function, yet they share common characteristics and essential components that enable them to perform life-sustaining activities like obtaining nutrients, growing, and reproducing.

Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Cells

Living cells are classified into two main types: prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. The primary distinction lies in the presence or absence of a membrane-bound nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.

Prokaryotic cells are generally simpler and smaller. They lack a true nucleus, meaning their genetic material (DNA) is not enclosed within a membrane but floats freely in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Prokaryotic cells also lack other membrane-bound organelles. Bacteria and archaea are examples of prokaryotic organisms.

Eukaryotic cells are typically larger and more complex. They possess a true nucleus, which houses the genetic material within a nuclear envelope. Eukaryotic cells also contain various membrane-bound organelles, each with specialised functions. Examples of eukaryotic organisms include plants, animals, fungi, and protists.

Despite their differences, both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells share some common features, such as a cell membrane, cytoplasm, and ribosomes.

Common Cell Structures and Organelles

Both plant and animal cells, as eukaryotic cells, share several key structures and organelles, each performing vital functions:

  • Cell membrane (Plasma membrane): This outer boundary of the cell is a selectively permeable barrier that controls the movement of substances into and out of the cell. It is composed of a lipid bilayer with embedded proteins.
  • Cytoplasm: The jelly-like substance that fills the cell, enclosing the organelles. It is the site of many metabolic reactions.
  • Nucleus: A large, usually spherical organelle that contains the cell's genetic material (chromosomes) in the form of DNA. It controls cell activities by regulating gene expression. The nucleus is enclosed by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope.
  • Chromosomes: Thread-like structures located inside the nucleus, composed of DNA tightly coiled around proteins. They carry genetic information in the form of genes.
  • Mitochondria (singular: mitochondrion): Often called the 'powerhouses' of the cell, mitochondria are responsible for cellular respiration, the process of converting nutrients into adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the cell's main energy currency.
  • Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER): A network of membranes that forms sacs and tubules throughout the cytoplasm. It plays a role in protein and lipid synthesis. There are two types: Rough ER (studded with ribosomes) involved in protein synthesis and modification, and Smooth ER (lacks ribosomes) involved in lipid synthesis, detoxification, and calcium storage.
  • Ribosomes: Small organelles responsible for protein synthesis. They can be found free in the cytoplasm or attached to the rough ER.
  • Golgi apparatus (Golgi complex/body): A stack of flattened membrane-bound sacs (cisternae) involved in modifying, sorting, and packaging proteins and lipids for secretion or delivery to other organelles.
  • Vacuoles: Membrane-bound sacs that can store water, nutrients, waste products, and other substances. Their size and function vary significantly between plant and animal cells.

Plant Cell Specific Structures and Comparisons

Plant cells possess several unique structures not found in animal cells, which are crucial for their specific functions, particularly photosynthesis and structural support. These differences highlight the distinct evolutionary paths and functional roles of plant and animal life.

Structures unique to plant cells:

  • Cell wall: A rigid outer layer found outside the cell membrane, primarily composed of cellulose. It provides structural support and protection to the plant cell, maintains its shape, and prevents excessive water uptake.
  • Chloroplasts: Oval-shaped organelles containing the green pigment chlorophyll. Chloroplasts are the sites of photosynthesis, the process by which light energy is converted into chemical energy (sugars).
  • Large central vacuole (Sap vacuole): A prominent, single, large membrane-bound sac that can occupy up to 90% of the cell volume. It stores water, nutrients, and waste products, maintains turgor pressure against the cell wall, and helps in cell growth.

Similarities between plant and animal cells:

Both plant and animal cells are eukaryotic and share fundamental organelles such as the cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and ribosomes.

Specialised Cells and Their Functions

In multicellular organisms, cells often undergo differentiation to become specialised cells, meaning they develop specific structures that enable them to perform particular functions more efficiently. This specialisation is crucial for the formation of tissues, organs, and organ systems.

Examples of specialised cells and their structure-function relationships:

  • Red blood cells (Erythrocytes): These cells are biconcave discs, which increases their surface area for oxygen absorption and allows them to squeeze through narrow capillaries. They lack a nucleus and mitochondria in mammals, freeing up space for haemoglobin, a protein that binds to and transports oxygen.
  • Neurones (Nerve cells): Characterised by a cell body, dendrites (branching extensions that receive signals), and a long axon (transmits signals over long distances). Their elongated structure and ability to generate electrical impulses allow for rapid communication throughout the body.
  • Ciliated cells: Found in the lining of the respiratory tract, these cells have hair-like projections called cilia on their surface. The rhythmic beating of cilia sweeps mucus, dust, and pathogens out of the airways, protecting the lungs.
  • Root hair cells: Located on the surface of plant roots, these cells have a long, thin extension called a root hair that greatly increases the surface area for efficient absorption of water and mineral ions from the soil. They also lack chloroplasts as they are underground.
  • Palisade cells: Found in the upper layer of plant leaves, these cells are elongated and tightly packed. They contain a large number of chloroplasts, which are arranged to maximise light absorption for photosynthesis.

Organisation of Cells: Tissues, Organs, and Systems

In multicellular organisms, cells are organised into higher levels of complexity to perform coordinated functions. This hierarchical organisation ensures the efficient functioning of the entire organism.

  1. Cells: The basic building blocks. Specialised cells perform specific tasks.
  2. Tissues: A group of similar cells that work together to perform a specific function. For example, muscle tissue (for contraction), nervous tissue (for communication), epithelial tissue (for lining and protection), and connective tissue (for support and binding).
  3. Organs: A structure made up of different types of tissues that work together to perform a more complex function. Examples include the heart (made of cardiac muscle, nervous, and connective tissues to pump blood), the stomach (made of muscle, epithelial, and nervous tissues for digestion), and a leaf (made of palisade, spongy, and vascular tissues for photosynthesis).
  4. Organ Systems: A group of organs that work together to perform major functions necessary for the survival of the organism. Examples include the digestive system (mouth, oesophagus, stomach, intestines), respiratory system (lungs, trachea), and circulatory system (heart, blood vessels, blood).

This intricate organisation from cells to organ systems allows complex multicellular organisms to carry out diverse and sophisticated life processes.

Salah faham biasa

Mitos All cells are the same shape and size.

Sebenarnya Cells exhibit a wide variety of shapes and sizes, which are adapted to their specific functions. For example, nerve cells are long and branched for signal transmission, while red blood cells are disc-shaped for oxygen transport.

Mitos Plants only have plant cells, and animals only have animal cells.

Sebenarnya While plants and animals have distinct cell types (plant cells and animal cells, respectively), both are types of eukaryotic cells. Furthermore, complex organisms often contain various specialised cells within their bodies, all derived from a common eukaryotic cell plan.

Mitos The cell wall is the same as the cell membrane.

Sebenarnya The cell wall and cell membrane are distinct structures. The cell wall is a rigid outer layer found only in plant cells (and some other organisms like fungi and bacteria), providing structural support. The cell membrane is a selectively permeable barrier found in all cells (plant, animal, prokaryotic) located just inside the cell wall in plants, or as the outermost boundary in animal cells.

Mitos All organisms have a nucleus.

Sebenarnya Only eukaryotic cells (found in plants, animals, fungi, and protists) have a true nucleus that encloses their genetic material. Prokaryotic cells (bacteria and archaea) lack a membrane-bound nucleus; their genetic material is located in the cytoplasm.

Mitos Chloroplasts are in all cells.

Sebenarnya Chloroplasts are specific to plant cells and some other photosynthetic organisms. They are the sites of photosynthesis. Animal cells do not contain chloroplasts as they obtain energy by consuming other organisms rather than performing photosynthesis.
