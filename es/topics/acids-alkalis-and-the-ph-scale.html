La explicación

Understanding Acids and Bases: Ionic Definitions and Properties

Acids and bases are two fundamental classes of chemical substances that play critical roles in chemistry and everyday life. An acid is a chemical species that, when dissolved in water, produces hydrogen ions (H+) as the only positively charged ions. Because a bare proton cannot exist independently in water, it associates with water molecules to form hydronium ions (H3O+), though chemists often write H+(aq) for simplicity. Acids typically taste sour, turn blue litmus paper red, and conduct electricity in aqueous solution due to the presence of mobile ions. Examples include hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulphuric acid (H2SO4), and nitric acid (HNO3), which are classified as strong mineral acids, as well as organic acids like acetic acid (CH3COOH) found in vinegar.

Bases are chemical compounds that react with acids to form salt and water. A base that is soluble in water is specifically termed an alkali. Alkalis dissolve in water to release hydroxide ions (OH-) as the only negatively charged ions. Examples of common alkalis include sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), and calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2). Bases generally have a bitter taste, feel soapy to the touch, and turn red litmus paper blue. In aqueous solutions, both acids and alkalis conduct electric currents because they dissociate into freely moving ions, a property known as electrolyte behavior.

Chemical Properties of Acids and Alkalis

The chemical behavior of acids and bases is dictated by their reactivity with metals, metal oxides, non-metal oxides, and carbonates. When a reactive metal such as zinc or magnesium reacts with a dilute acid, it displaces hydrogen, producing a metal salt and hydrogen gas. For example, zinc reacting with dilute hydrochloric acid yields zinc chloride and hydrogen gas, which can be identified by a pop sound when brought near a burning splint.

Acids react with metal oxides and metal hydroxides to form salt and water, a process that mirrors neutralisation. Similarly, acids react with metal carbonates and metal hydrogencarbonates to yield a salt, water, and carbon dioxide gas, which turns lime water milky due to the formation of insoluble calcium carbonate. Alkalis exhibit distinct reactions as well; for instance, strong alkalis react with certain amphoteric metals like zinc or aluminium to release hydrogen gas and form soluble complex salts.

Bases and acids undergo neutralisation when mixed. In a neutralisation reaction, an acid reacts with a base to neutralize each other's properties, producing a salt and water accompanied by the release of heat. The net ionic equation for a neutralisation reaction between a strong acid and a strong alkali is the combination of hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions to form water molecules.

Indicators and the pH Scale

To determine whether a substance is acidic, basic, or neutral, chemists use indicators. Indicators are dyes or mixtures of dyes that change colour depending on the acidity or alkalinity of the medium they are placed in. Natural indicators include litmus (extracted from lichens), turmeric, and red cabbage extract. Synthetic indicators include methyl orange and phenolphthalein. Methyl orange turns red in acidic solutions and yellow in basic solutions, whereas phenolphthalein is colourless in acidic solutions and turns pink in alkaline solutions.

While indicators show whether a solution is acidic or basic, the pH scale provides a precise quantitative measure of the degree of acidity or alkalinity. The term pH stands for the potential of hydrogen. The pH scale typically ranges from 0 to 14. A pH value of exactly 7 represents a neutral solution, such as pure water at standard temperature. Values less than 7 indicate an acidic solution, with lower numbers representing higher acidity and greater concentration of hydrogen ions. Values greater than 7 indicate an alkaline solution, with higher numbers representing higher alkalinity and greater concentration of hydroxide ions.

A Universal Indicator is a mixture of several indicators that exhibits a smooth spectrum of colour changes over the entire pH range, allowing a researcher to estimate the exact pH of a solution by matching the resulting colour to a standard chart. Strong acids have very low pH values near 0 or 1, while strong alkalis have very high pH values near 13 or 14. Weak acids have pH values closer to 5 or 6, and weak alkalis have pH values around 8 or 9.

Importance of pH in Everyday Life

The concept of pH is not merely a laboratory curiosity; it governs numerous biological, environmental, and domestic phenomena. Living organisms are sensitive to pH changes and generally operate within a narrow pH range. The human body functions best within a pH range of 7.0 to 7.8, and even minor fluctuations can disrupt metabolic processes. When acid rain lowers the pH of river water below 5.6, aquatic life survival becomes difficult.

In the digestive system, the human stomach produces hydrochloric acid, which aids in food digestion without harming the stomach lining due to a protective mucus layer. During indigestion, the stomach produces excess acid, causing pain and irritation. This can be neutralised using mild bases called antacids, such as milk of magnesia (Mg(OH)2).

Tooth decay is another consequence of pH fluctuations. Bacteria present in the mouth degrade leftover food particles, producing acids that lower the pH below 5.5. This acidic environment corrodes tooth enamel, which is made of calcium phosphate. Regular cleaning with alkaline toothpaste helps neutralise the acid and prevent decay.

Plants also exhibit pH preferences. Soil pH influences nutrient availability, and most plants thrive in a neutral to slightly acidic or basic soil (pH 6.5 to 7.3). If soil is too acidic due to acid rain or excessive fertiliser use, farmers add basic substances like quicklime (CaO) or slaked lime (Ca(OH)2) to raise the pH. Conversely, alkaline soils are treated with organic matter or sulphur to lower the pH. Additionally, certain insects and plants, such as stinging nettles, inject acidic chemicals like methanoic acid into the skin upon contact, causing pain that can be relieved by applying a weak base like baking soda.

Preparation and Uses of Important Industrial Salts

Chemical manufacturing processes convert common salt and other raw materials into essential industrial chemicals and salts. The Chlor-Alkali process involves the electrolysis of an aqueous solution of sodium chloride, known as brine. During this process, electricity is passed through the brine, causing it to decompose into sodium hydroxide (NaOH) at the cathode, chlorine gas at the anode, and hydrogen gas at the cathode. Sodium hydroxide is widely used for soap and detergent manufacturing, paper making, and petroleum refining, while chlorine gas is used for water disinfection and producing PVC.

Bleaching powder (calcium oxychloride, CaOCl2) is produced by the action of chlorine gas (obtained from the chlor-alkali process) on dry slaked lime (Ca(OH)2). It is extensively used for bleaching cotton and linen in the textile industry, bleaching wood pulp in paper factories, and as an oxidizing agent in chemical industries.

Baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO3) is produced using the Solvay process, involving the reaction of sodium chloride, water, carbon dioxide, and ammonia. When heated during cooking, baking soda decomposes to release carbon dioxide gas, which causes cakes and bread to rise, making them soft and spongy. It is also a key ingredient in antacids due to its mild alkaline nature.

Washing soda (sodium carbonate decahydrate, Na2CO3.10H2O) is obtained by recrystallisation of sodium carbonate, which is itself produced by heating baking soda. Washing soda is used in glass, soap, and paper industries, in manufacturing borax, and for softening hard water.

Plaster of Paris (calcium sulphate hemihydrate, CaSO4.0.5H2O) is prepared by heating gypsum (CaSO4.2H2O) at 373 Kelvin. At this temperature, gypsum loses water molecules to form plaster of Paris. When mixed with water, plaster of Paris sets into a hard solid mass by rehydrating back into gypsum, making it invaluable for setting fractured bones, making decorative casts, and smoothing surfaces.

Términos clave

Acid
A substance that releases hydrogen ions (H+) in aqueous solution, has a sour taste, and turns blue litmus red.
Alkali
A base that is soluble in water and releases hydroxide ions (OH-) in aqueous solution.
Indicator
A dye that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acidic or basic environment.
pH Scale
A logarithmic scale from 0 to 14 used to specify the acidity or alkalinity of an aqueous solution.
Neutralisation
The chemical reaction between an acid and a base that produces a salt and water.
Chlor-Alkali Process
The electrolysis of aqueous sodium chloride that produces sodium hydroxide, chlorine gas, and hydrogen gas.
Bleaching Powder
Calcium oxychloride (CaOCl2), used as a bleaching agent and disinfectant.
Plaster of Paris
Calcium sulphate hemihydrate, formed by heating gypsum, which sets hard upon mixing with water.

Ejemplos resueltos

1Explain the chemical changes that occur when baking soda is heated during the baking of a cake, and write the balanced chemical equation.

Mostrar la solución
  1. Identify the chemical formula of baking soda: Sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO3).
  2. Understand the effect of heat: Upon heating during baking, sodium hydrogencarbonate undergoes thermal decomposition.
  3. Write the products: The products formed are sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), water vapour (H2O), and carbon dioxide gas (CO2).
  4. Balance the equation: 2NaHCO3 (s) --(Heat)--> Na2CO3 (s) + H2O (g) + CO2 (g).
  5. Relate to application: The trapped carbon dioxide gas bubbles expand and cause dough to rise, making cakes spongy.

2Calculate the amount of water of crystallisation lost when 2 moles of gypsum are converted into plaster of Paris.

Mostrar la solución
  1. Identify the chemical formula of gypsum: CaSO4.2H2O.
  2. Identify the chemical formula of plaster of Paris: CaSO4.0.5H2O (or 2CaSO4.H2O).
  3. Write the conversion reaction: 2[CaSO4.2H2O] --(Heat)--> [2CaSO4.H2O] + 3H2O.
  4. Interpret the moles: Heating 2 moles of gypsum releases 3 moles of water molecules.

Ideas erróneas frecuentes

Error común All compounds containing hydrogen atoms are classified as acids.

En realidad Only compounds that release hydrogen ions (H+) when dissolved in water, such as HCl, act as acids; substances like methane (CH4) contain hydrogen but do not release H+ ions in water.

Error común All bases are alkalis.

En realidad An alkali is specifically a base that dissolves in water; many bases, such as copper oxide, are insoluble in water and therefore are not alkalis.

Error común Water is always strictly neutral with a pH of 7.

En realidad The pH of pure water varies with temperature due to changes in its self-ionisation equilibrium; it is only neutral (equal concentrations of H+ and OH-) at standard temperature.

Error común Neutralisation always results in a neutral solution of pH 7.

En realidad The pH at the equivalence point of a neutralisation reaction depends on the strengths of the reacting acid and base; a strong acid and weak base yield an acidic salt solution with a pH below 7.
