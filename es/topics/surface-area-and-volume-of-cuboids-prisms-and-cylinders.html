La explicación

Introduction to Three-Dimensional Solids and Nets

Three-dimensional objects, or 3D shapes, occupy space and possess length, width and height. Unlike two-dimensional shapes which exist on a flat plane, 3D solids have faces, edges and vertices. A face is a flat surface bound by edges, an edge is a line segment where two faces meet, and a vertex is a corner point where edges intersect.

Understanding the structure of a 3D solid is greatly assisted by examining its net. A net is a two-dimensional pattern that can be folded along its edges to create a three-dimensional solid. Visualising or drawing a net allows every individual face of the solid to be laid out flat, showing its true shape and dimensions. For example, a cube unfolds into a net consisting of six identical squares, while a rectangular cuboid unfolds into a net of six rectangles arranged in three opposite identical pairs. A cylinder unfolds into a net containing two identical circles (the bases) and one rectangle (the curved lateral surface, where the length of the rectangle matches the circumference of the circular base).

Surface area calculations rely directly on understanding these constituent faces. The total surface area of any polyhedron or curved solid is the sum of the areas of all the individual faces that enclose the solid. By breaking a solid down into its net, calculating the area of each component flat shape using standard area formulas, and adding those areas together, the total external surface area is determined without needing complex multi-variable calculus.

Surface Area of Cubes, Cuboids and Triangular Prisms

Calculating the surface area of a cube or cuboid requires finding the area of all six rectangular (or square) faces and finding their sum. A cube has six identical square faces of side length s. Therefore, the total surface area of a cube is given by the formula six multiplied by the side length squared.

A cuboid has length l, width w and height h. It has three pairs of identical rectangular faces: top and bottom (each with area l times w), front and back (each with area l times h), and left and right sides (each with area w times h). Consequently, the total surface area of a cuboid is the sum of the areas of these three distinct pairs, expressed as two multiplied by the sum of length times width plus length times height plus width times height.

A triangular prism is a polyhedron made of a triangular base, a congruent and parallel triangular top, and three rectangular sides connecting corresponding edges. Its surface area is the sum of the areas of the two identical triangular faces and the three rectangular faces. The dimensions of the three rectangles depend on the side lengths of the triangle and the length or height of the prism itself. When calculating surface area from a drawing or net, each face must be systematically identified, its dimensions extracted, its area calculated, and all areas summed together.

Surface Area of Right Circular Cylinders

A right circular cylinder is a solid bounded by two parallel circular bases of equal radius r and a curved surface perpendicular to the circular bases connecting them. The total surface area of a cylinder consists of three parts: the top circular face, the bottom circular face, and the curved lateral surface area.

The area of one circular base is pi multiplied by the radius squared. Since there are two identical circular bases, their combined area is two multiplied by pi multiplied by the radius squared.

The curved surface, when unrolled into a flat net, forms a rectangle. One dimension of this rectangle is the height (or length) of the cylinder, denoted by h. The other dimension must wrap completely around the circular base, meaning its length is equal to the circumference of the circle, which is two multiplied by pi multiplied by the radius. Therefore, the area of the curved surface is two multiplied by pi multiplied by the radius multiplied by the height of the cylinder.

Summing the areas of the two circular bases and the curved rectangle yields the formula for the total surface area of a cylinder: two multiplied by pi multiplied by the radius squared plus two multiplied by pi multiplied by the radius multiplied by the height. Factoring this expression provides a concise form: two multiplied by pi multiplied by the radius multiplied by the sum of the radius and the height.

Derivation and Calculation of Volume for Prisms and Cylinders

The volume of a solid measures the three-dimensional space it occupies, expressed in cubic units such as cubic centimetres or cubic metres. A prism is a solid with a constant cross-section along its entire length. A cross-section is the shape exposed by making a straight cut parallel to the base faces.

To derive the volume formula for a prism, consider stacking identical flat layers or cross-sections on top of one another. If a single cross-section has an area of A square units, and the prism extends for a perpendicular length or height of l units, the volume is built up by multiplying the cross-sectional area by the number of units of length. This yields the universal prism volume formula: volume equals the cross-sectional area multiplied by the length (or height).

For a cuboid, the cross-section is a rectangle of area length times width. Multiplying by the height gives the standard cuboid volume formula: length multiplied by width multiplied by height.

For a triangular prism, the cross-section is a triangle. The cross-sectional area is found using half multiplied by the base of the triangle multiplied by the perpendicular height of the triangle. Multiplying this triangular area by the length of the prism gives its volume.

A cylinder can be conceptualised as a prism whose cross-section is a circle. The area of the circular cross-section is pi multiplied by the radius squared. Applying the universal prism principle, the volume of a cylinder is obtained by multiplying the circular cross-sectional area by the height of the cylinder. Thus, the volume of a cylinder equals pi multiplied by the radius squared multiplied by the height.

Ejemplos resueltos

1Calculate the total surface area and the volume of a cuboid with a length of 8 cm, a width of 5 cm, and a height of 3 cm.

Mostrar la solución

Step 1: Identify the formula for the surface area of a cuboid: Total Surface Area = 2(lw + lh + wh).

Step 2: Substitute the given values (l = 8, w = 5, h = 3):

  • lw = 8 * 5 = 40
  • lh = 8 * 3 = 24
  • wh = 5 * 3 = 15

Step 3: Sum these products and multiply by 2:

  • Surface Area = 2(40 + 24 + 15) = 2(79) = 158 square centimetres.

Step 4: Identify the formula for the volume of a cuboid: Volume = l w h.

Step 5: Substitute the values:

  • Volume = 8 5 3 = 120 cubic centimetres.

2Find the volume and total surface area of a right circular cylinder with a radius of 4 cm and a height of 10 cm. Use pi approximately equal to 3.14.

Mostrar la solución

Step 1: Calculate the volume of the cylinder using the formula Volume = pi r2 h.

Step 2: Substitute r = 4 and h = 10:

  • Volume = 3.14 (42) 10
  • Volume = 3.14 16 10
  • Volume = 502.4 cubic centimetres.

Step 3: Calculate the total surface area using the formula Total Surface Area = 2 pi r2 + 2 pi r * h.

Step 4: Calculate the area of the two circular bases: 2 3.14 (42) = 2 3.14 16 = 100.48 square centimetres.

Step 5: Calculate the curved surface area: 2 3.14 4 * 10 = 251.2 square centimetres.

Step 6: Add the base areas and curved surface area together:

  • Total Surface Area = 100.48 + 251.2 = 351.68 square centimetres.

Ideas erróneas frecuentes

Error común Learners often think the volume formula for a cylinder is identical to its curved surface area formula, confusing pi times radius squared times height with two times pi times radius times height.

En realidad Volume measures internal space in cubic units using the cross-sectional area times height (pi times radius squared times height), whereas curved surface area measures flat exterior area in square units (two times pi times radius times height).

Error común When calculating the surface area of a cuboid, learners sometimes multiply all three dimensions together as length times width times height.

En realidad Multiplying length, width, and height calculates volume, not surface area. Surface area is found by calculating the area of all six separate rectangular faces and adding them together.

Error común Learners may assume that the height of a triangular prism is always the vertical height of the triangular face, regardless of how the prism is oriented.

En realidad A prism has two distinct heights: the height (or length) of the prism itself which separates the two identical cross-sectional bases, and the height of the triangular cross-section itself. Both must be correctly distinguished when calculating volume or surface area.

Error común When given the diameter of a cylinder in a problem, learners sometimes use the diameter directly in place of the radius in volume and surface area formulas.

En realidad The radius is strictly half of the diameter. The radius must always be calculated by dividing the diameter by two before substituting it into formulas involving radius squared.
