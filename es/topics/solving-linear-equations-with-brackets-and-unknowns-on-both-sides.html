La explicación

Foundations of Linear Equations

A linear equation is a mathematical statement asserting that two algebraic expressions are equal, where the highest power of any variable involved is one. Solving such an equation means finding the exact numerical value of the unknown variable that makes the equality true. The process relies on maintaining balance across the equals sign, treating the equation like a traditional beam balance where whatever operation is performed on one side must be performed identically on the other side. Before tackling more complex equations that contain brackets and variables on both sides, learners must be fluent in the fundamental rules of balancing, combining like terms, and performing inverse operations.

Inverse operations are pairs of mathematical operations that undo each other. Addition and subtraction are inverses, while multiplication and division are inverses. When solving an equation, the ultimate goal is to isolate the unknown variable on one side and the numerical constants on the other side. This isolation is achieved by systematically applying inverse operations to peel away numbers attached to the variable. For example, if an equation has a term being added, subtracting that term from both sides eliminates it from the original side. Similarly, multiplying or dividing both sides by a common non-zero factor preserves the equality while altering the coefficients of the terms.

Like terms are terms that contain the exact same variable raised to the exact same power. In linear equations, grouping like terms simplifies the algebraic landscape, making the isolation of the variable much clearer. For instance, terms containing the variable x can be combined by adding or subtracting their coefficients, while pure numerical constants are combined separately. Maintaining accuracy with negative numbers during this grouping phase is vital, as a single sign error will propagate through the entire calculation and yield an incorrect final solution. The systematic application of these foundational rules underpins every advanced algebraic manipulation, including the expansion of brackets and the management of unknowns appearing on both sides of the equals sign.

Expanding Brackets Using the Distributive Law

Brackets appear in algebraic expressions to indicate that a multiplier outside the grouping symbol must be applied to every single term located inside the grouping symbol. This operation is governed by the distributive law of multiplication over addition and subtraction. Mathematically, for any algebraic terms a, b, and c, the expression a multiplied by the sum of b and c equals a times b plus a times c. When a negative number or a negative sign sits outside the bracket, particular care must be taken with the multiplication rules of signed numbers, as multiplying a negative by a negative yields a positive, and multiplying a positive by a negative yields a negative.

To expand a bracket cleanly, the term on the outside is multiplied in turn by each separate term on the inside. For example, in the expression 3 multiplied by the sum of 2x and 5, the outside factor 3 multiplies 2x to give 6x, and then multiplies positive 5 to give positive 15, resulting in the expanded form 6x plus 15. If the multiplier is negative, such as negative 2 multiplying the difference of 4x and 3, the expansion yields negative 8x plus 6 because negative 2 multiplied by negative 3 produces positive 6. Brackets must always be expanded fully before attempting to collect like terms or rearrange equations across the equals sign.

Failing to multiply every term inside the bracket is a frequent source of error in algebraic manipulation. Learners must ensure that if a bracket contains three terms, the outside factor is distributed to all three terms without exception. Additionally, numbers that sit outside the brackets but are not multiplied by them must not be accidentally absorbed into the expansion. For instance, in the expression 4 plus 2 multiplied by the sum of x and 3, the number 4 remains separate, and only the 2 is distributed across the bracket, yielding 4 plus 2x plus 6, which then simplifies to 2x plus 10.

Collecting Unknowns on Both Sides

Many advanced linear equations feature the unknown variable appearing on both the left-hand side and the right-hand side of the equals sign. To solve such equations, all terms containing the unknown must be gathered onto a single side, while all constant numerical terms are gathered onto the opposite side. It does not matter mathematically whether the unknown terms are collected on the left side or the right side, though convention and ease of reading often favour moving the smaller coefficient of the unknown toward the side with the larger coefficient to maintain positive coefficients wherever possible.

To move an unknown term from one side of the equation to the other, the inverse operation is applied to both sides. If an equation contains a positive 3x on the right-hand side, subtracting 3x from both sides effectively removes it from the right and introduces a minus 3x term on the left. Once all the variable terms reside on one side, they are combined by adding or subtracting their coefficients. Simultaneously, constant terms are moved to the opposite side using their respective inverse operations until the equation is reduced to a simple two-step form consisting of a single coefficient multiplied by the unknown equal to a single constant value.

Maintaining rigorous step-by-step documentation during this collection process prevents confusion. Writing out each intermediate line clearly ensures that signs are not accidentally flipped when crossing the equals sign. After collecting the unknowns and constants, a final division or multiplication by the coefficient of the unknown yields the exact value of the variable. Checking this final answer by substituting it back into the original equation confirms its validity and ensures that no arithmetic slips occurred during the rearrangement stages.

Step-by-Step Worked Examples

Applying the principles of bracket expansion, variable collection, and inverse operations requires a disciplined, methodical sequence of written steps. The first worked example demonstrates an equation with brackets on one side and unknowns on both sides. Consider the equation 5 multiplied by the sum of x and 2 equals 3x plus 18. The initial step is to expand the bracket on the left-hand side by distributing the 5, yielding 5x plus 10 equals 3x plus 18. Next, the variable terms are gathered on the left by subtracting 3x from both sides, which transforms the equation into 2x plus 10 equals 18. Subtracting 10 from both sides isolates the variable term, giving 2x equals 8. Finally, dividing both sides by 2 reveals the solution x equals 4. Substituting 4 back into the original equation checks out, as 5 times the sum of 4 and 2 equals 30, and 3 times 4 plus 18 equals 30.

The second worked example features brackets on both sides of the equation and negative numbers. Consider the equation 4 multiplied by the difference of x and 1 equals 2 multiplied by the sum of x and 5. Expanding both brackets gives 4x minus 4 equals 2x plus 10. To collect the unknowns on the left, subtract 2x from both sides, resulting in 2x minus 4 equals 10. Adding 4 to both sides gives 2x equals 14. Dividing by 2 yields x equals 7. Checking this result, the left side becomes 4 times 6, which is 24, and the right side becomes 2 times 12, which is 24, confirming complete accuracy.

The third worked example incorporates negative outside multipliers, which test sign discipline. Consider 3 minus 2 multiplied by the sum of x and 1 equals 5 minus x. Expanding the bracket with the negative multiplier requires caution: 3 minus 2x minus 2 equals 5 minus x. Simplifying the left-hand side constants gives 1 minus 2x equals 5 minus x. Adding 2x to both sides to keep the variable coefficient positive yields 1 equals 5 plus x. Subtracting 5 from both sides gives negative 4 equals x, or x equals negative 4. Verification confirms that both sides evaluate to 9.

Common Misconceptions and How to Avoid Them

Algebraic problem-solving is prone to specific recurrent errors that arise from misinterpreting rules of signs, bracket expansion, and equation balancing. One major misconception is distributing only the numerical coefficient to the first term inside a bracket while ignoring the second term. For example, expanding 3 multiplied by the sum of x and 4 incorrectly as 3x plus 4 instead of 3x plus 12. To avoid this error, learners should draw visual arrows linking the outside multiplier to every individual term inside the bracket before writing down the expanded expression.

Another frequent error involves sign management when a negative number multiplies a bracket or when terms cross the equals sign. Learners often forget that multiplying a negative term by a negative term produces a positive result, leading to sign errors in expansion. Furthermore, when moving a term across the equals sign, students sometimes neglect to change its sign, treating addition as if it remains addition rather than converting to subtraction. The golden rule is that every term crossing the equals sign must undergo its inverse operation.

A third misconception occurs when variables are cancelled incorrectly or when coefficients are mishandled during collection. Some learners attempt to divide the entire equation by the variable itself rather than collecting terms, which destroys the linear structure and eliminates valid roots. Others add coefficients across the equals sign instead of performing inverse operations. Maintaining strict adherence to balancing principles—performing identical inverse operations to both sides simultaneously—prevents these structural errors and ensures reliable solutions.

Términos clave

Linear Equation
An algebraic equation in which the highest power of the unknown variable is one.
Bracket Expansion
The process of multiplying a single outside term by every term inside a grouping symbol using the distributive law.
Inverse Operation
An operation that reverses the effect of another operation, such as subtraction undoing addition.
Like Terms
Terms that contain the exact same variable raised to the exact same power.
Distributive Law
The rule stating that multiplying a number by a sum gives the same result as multiplying the number by each term in the sum and adding the products.

Ejemplos resueltos

1Solve the equation: 5(x + 2) = 3x + 18

Mostrar la solución

Step 1: Expand the bracket on the left-hand side by distributing 5.

5x + 10 = 3x + 18

Step 2: Collect the unknown terms on the left by subtracting 3x from both sides.

5x - 3x + 10 = 18

2x + 10 = 18

Step 3: Isolate the variable term by subtracting 10 from both sides.

2x = 18 - 10

2x = 8

Step 4: Divide both sides by 2 to find the value of x.

x = 8 / 2

x = 4

2Solve the equation: 4(x - 1) = 2(x + 5)

Mostrar la solución

Step 1: Expand both brackets using the distributive law.

4x - 4 = 2x + 10

Step 2: Collect the unknown terms on the left by subtracting 2x from both sides.

4x - 2x - 4 = 10

2x - 4 = 10

Step 3: Isolate the variable term by adding 4 to both sides.

2x = 10 + 4

2x = 14

Step 4: Divide both sides by 2 to solve for x.

x = 14 / 2

x = 7

Ideas erróneas frecuentes

Error común Multiplying only the first term inside a bracket by the outside number.

En realidad The outside multiplier must be distributed to every single term located inside the bracket without exception.

Error común Forgetting to change the sign of a term when moving it across the equals sign.

En realidad Moving any term across the equals sign requires applying the complete inverse operation, changing addition to subtraction and vice versa.

Error común Treating a negative outside multiplier as positive during bracket expansion.

En realidad A negative multiplier must be distributed across all internal terms, reversing their signs appropriately according to the rules of signed numbers.

Error común Attempting to divide the entire equation by the variable to eliminate it from one side.

En realidad Variables on both sides must be collected by adding or subtracting term coefficients rather than dividing by the unknown variable.
